The emails we don't send yet

Loopinglive sends 17 transactional emails. Every one of them is addressed to the host's audience. None are addressed to the host — the person who actually pays for the product. That single asymmetry is where most of the missing value sits.

A webinar platform's whole value is show-up rate, and we ship no calendar invite. Verified: no .ics generation exists anywhere in the codebase. It is the single highest-leverage thing missing, and it is a few days of work.

Correctness, not features

These are cases where the product silently does the wrong thing. They damage the host's reputation with their own audience, which is the worst failure mode for a B2B tool. I'd treat them as bugs and fix them before building anything new.

Trust & integrity Blocking 3 emails
  • Session rescheduled A host can change a session time in the schedule editor and registrants are never told. They arrive to nothing, or miss it entirely.
  • Session cancelled Same gap, worse outcome. Currently the only signal is silence.
  • Video processing failed Uploads are async through Cloudinary. If transcoding fails, the webinar is broken and nobody learns until showtime.

Attendance lift

The metric the entire product lives on. A percentage point here is worth more than most features, because it compounds across every host and every session.

Getting people into the room Highest ROI 4 emails
  • Calendar invite (.ics) on registration Attaching an .ics plus a Google Calendar link is the standard lever for show-up rate. Every serious competitor does this; its absence is conspicuous.
  • Add-to-calendar in every reminder Not just the confirmation — people decide to attend at the 24h and 1h marks.
  • Timezone-confirmed restatement A time shown in the wrong timezone is a total loss, not a partial one. Worth an explicit confirmation of the reader's zone.
  • Waitlist / session full Only if capacity becomes a concept — but it also manufactures scarcity, which suits this product.

Host activation

The activation event is first published webinar that gets registrants. Everything here exists to shorten the path to that moment, then prove it worked.

Signup → active customer High 6 emails
  • Welcome & first run Currently nothing is sent at signup at all.
  • Abandoned setup nudge We already store the setup checklist, so this can be specific rather than generic: "3 steps left — personas, offer, AI moderators."
  • First webinar published Hand them the share link and a nudge to promote it.
  • First registrant "Sarah just registered." Proof the thing works, delivered the moment it does.
  • First sale The strongest retention moment the product has. It should be impossible to miss.
  • Registrant milestones 10th, 100th, 1000th. Cheap to build, disproportionately motivating.

Host retention through value

Phase 6 already computes all of this. These emails are mostly plumbing, and they deliver the product's insight without requiring anyone to log in — which is exactly how analytics earns its keep.

Bringing the numbers to them High · cheap 4 emails
  • Session recap Fires when a session ends: attended, watch depth, offer clicks, revenue, and the biggest drop-off moment. The retention workhorse for this product.
  • Weekly digest Across every webinar, with week-on-week deltas — the rollup tables already hold this.
  • Pre-session heads-up "Starts in an hour, 42 registered." Gives the host a reason to show up to their own event.
  • Low-registration warning "Tomorrow's session has 0 registrants" — with something actionable attached, while there is still time.

What I'd build, in order

Sequenced by value per day of work, not by category. The first three are all small.

  1. Calendar invite

    The highest return in the product. It lifts the one metric every host is judged on, applies to all 17 existing emails' worth of traffic, and needs no new infrastructure — an .ics attachment and a Google Calendar link.

  2. Schedule change notifications

    Closes a trust bug rather than adding a feature. A host changing a time today produces silence, and their audience pays for it.

  3. Session recap to the host

    Phase 6 already computes every number. This is mostly plumbing, and it is the email most likely to make a host feel the product is working for them.

  4. Branded auth emails

    Replaces the cheapest-looking surface in the product, and moves those messages onto a domain we control and can monitor.

  5. Host activation series

    Abandoned setup, first registrant, first sale. The setup checklist already gives the abandoned-setup email something specific to say.

  6. Billing & dunning

    Ships with Phase 7. Involuntary churn is the cheapest churn to prevent, and it needs the sequence to exist on day one, not later.

Three decisions to make before the count grows

These are architectural, and each gets materially more expensive the longer the email surface expands without them.

Every host currently rides our sending reputation

All mail leaves as noreply@loopinglive.com. One host with a poor list degrades deliverability for every other host on the platform — and we saw a preview of this when a single bad address hard-bounced and was silently suppressed. Per-host sending domains isolate that risk and are a premium feature in their own right.

Cost of delay: rises with every new host

Unsubscribe is all-or-nothing

One link opts a person out of everything. At roughly 65 emails, that is untenable both practically and legally — transactional and marketing mail have to be separable, and a reminder about a session someone registered for is not marketing. A preference centre is the fix, and retrofitting one across 65 templates is far harder than designing it now.

Cost of delay: retrofit across every template

Bounces are invisible to the host

Resend suppresses a hard-bounced address automatically and permanently. Later sends return a normal message id and are never delivered. A host reading their dashboard believes they are reaching people they are not — and nothing in the product tells them otherwise. Bounce state needs to surface on the attendee list.

Cost of delay: silent data quality rot
